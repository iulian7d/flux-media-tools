How we build and verify our calculators

Where the numbers come from, how each tool is tested, and how we keep it current.

Every calculator on this site follows the same discipline: it is built from an official published figure, it shows the rule it applies, and it tells you the tax year and jurisdiction it covers. This page explains exactly how that works, so you can judge for yourself how much to trust a result — and check our working.

Where our numbers come from

We use primary, official sources — never a figure copied from another calculator or a forum. For UK money and property tools that means, depending on the calculation:

  • HMRC — income tax bands, National Insurance, Stamp Duty Land Tax rates and reliefs, Inheritance Tax thresholds and the nil-rate bands.
  • gov.uk — statutory redundancy pay, the weekly pay cap, notice-period rules, and the National Minimum/Living Wage.
  • DWP and the State Pension — State Pension amounts and the triple-lock uprating.
  • Bank of England — the base rate, used for the statutory late-payment interest calculation (base rate plus the fixed statutory margin).
  • ONS — where an inflation or average-earnings figure is needed for context.

The specific source and the year it applies to are stated on each calculator page, so you are never guessing which rules a number reflects.

How a calculator gets built

Each tool goes through the same four steps before it is published:

  • 1. Read the actual rule. We start from the official guidance or legislation — the real thresholds, bands, tapers, and exceptions — not a summary of it.
  • 2. Implement the exact formula. The maths mirrors the published method: correct bands in the correct order, reliefs applied where they legally apply, and rounding handled the way the source body does it.
  • 3. Test against known answers. We run the calculator against worked examples from the source — and, where the government publishes its own calculator (Stamp Duty, take-home pay, redundancy pay), we check our result matches theirs across a range of inputs, including the awkward edge cases at band boundaries.
  • 4. Document it. The page states the data used, the tax year, and the limits of what the tool can tell you.

How we test for accuracy

A calculator is only useful if it is right at the edges, not just in the middle. We test each tool at the points where mistakes usually hide: the exact thresholds where a tax band changes, the first pound over a relief limit, zero and very large inputs, and the differences between England, Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland where the rules diverge. Where an official calculator exists, matching it is the bar we hold ourselves to.

How we keep them up to date

Tax thresholds, benefit rates and statutory caps change — usually each April, sometimes mid-year. The redundancy weekly pay cap is reviewed every April; Stamp Duty thresholds in England changed in April 2025; the State Pension is uprated annually. We review the affected calculators against each year's figures and update them as the new rates take effect. Where a rate is known to be changing on a future date, the tool notes both the current and the upcoming figure. Where there is genuine uncertainty — an open consultation, a rate not yet confirmed — we flag it rather than show a falsely confident number.

What our calculators can't do

A calculator applies a formula to your numbers. It cannot know the specifics of your situation. Our Stamp Duty tool applies the published rates — it cannot decide whether a property qualifies for mixed-use treatment. Our redundancy tool applies the statutory formula — it cannot tell you whether your redundancy was genuine or your employer followed a fair process. These are questions for a qualified professional. Everything here is general information to get you to the right ballpark quickly, so that when you do speak to an accountant, adviser or solicitor, you arrive informed rather than starting from zero.

Corrections

Rates change and mistakes happen. If a figure looks wrong or out of date, the contact page comes straight to us and we correct errors quickly. Corrections are genuinely appreciated — they make the tools better for everyone.
