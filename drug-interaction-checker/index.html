How to Use This Checker

Enter the generic or brand names of any medications, vitamins, or supplements you take. The checker uses the OpenFDA drug label database to identify known interactions.

Common drug interaction types: Some drugs affect how quickly the body processes other drugs (CYP450 interactions). Others compete for the same receptors. Some can cause dangerous additive effects when combined (e.g., blood thinners, CNS depressants).

This database covers FDA-regulated drugs. Herbal supplements and vitamins may not appear in all cases. For a comprehensive review of all your medications, consult your pharmacist — they have access to professional-grade interaction databases.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a drug interaction?

A drug interaction occurs when one substance affects how another drug works in the body. Interactions can make a drug more potent (increasing side effects or toxicity), less effective (reducing therapeutic benefit), or cause entirely new effects not seen with either drug alone. Interactions can occur between prescription drugs, over-the-counter medications, vitamins, supplements, and even certain foods like grapefruit.

What are the most dangerous types of drug interactions?

The most serious interactions include: combining blood thinners (warfarin) with NSAIDs like ibuprofen, which increases bleeding risk; mixing CNS depressants like opioids, benzodiazepines, and alcohol, which can cause respiratory depression; combining MAOIs with SSRIs, which can cause serotonin syndrome; and using certain antibiotics with statins, which can cause muscle damage (rhabdomyolysis).

Can vitamins and supplements interact with medications?

Yes — many supplements have significant drug interactions. St. John's Wort reduces the effectiveness of many drugs including antidepressants, blood thinners, and birth control pills. Fish oil and vitamin E can increase bleeding risk with anticoagulants. Magnesium and calcium can reduce absorption of some antibiotics. Always tell your doctor and pharmacist about all supplements you take.

How do I find my medication's generic name?

Generic names are listed on your prescription label and pharmacy packaging, usually in smaller text beneath the brand name. You can also search online for "brand name generic equivalent" or check your patient information leaflet. Generic names are often more useful for interaction checking because one brand name drug can have multiple active ingredients.

Should I stop taking a medication if I find an interaction?

Never stop taking a prescribed medication without consulting your doctor or pharmacist first. Many interactions are manageable through dose adjustments, timing changes, or monitoring. Your prescriber will have weighed the benefits and risks when prescribing. If you're concerned, call your pharmacist — they are the most accessible healthcare professional for medication questions and can review your full medication list.
